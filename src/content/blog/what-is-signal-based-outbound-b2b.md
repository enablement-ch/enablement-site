**Signal-based outbound is B2B outreach triggered or prioritized by a verifiable observation that may indicate a buyer's pain, need, interest, or intent.** Instead of contacting every account on a list with the same message, you look for a reason to start a conversation, check that the account fits, and respond with something relevant.

The word *may* matters. A funding announcement, a website visit, or a comment on a LinkedIn post is an observation. It is not proof that someone wants a sales call. The work is to understand what the observation suggests, how confident you are, and what action it warrants.

At Enablement, we organize signals into three practical groups: activity around your own company, activity around the category you sell into, and broader changes in the market. The closer a signal is to a buyer's actual problem, the more useful it can be. The wider the pool of signals, the more care you need in interpreting them.

## The three signal types

| Signal type | What you can observe | What it may tell you | Typical volume | Sensible first action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **First party: engagement with you** | A webinar signup, content download, relevant site activity, product interaction, or engagement with your company or executives' content | They know something about your company and may be exploring a related problem | Lower | Check account fit and the specific activity, then offer a relevant next step |
| **Second party: category engagement** | Activity in a relevant event, community, review site, forum, or discussion about the category | They are exploring the problem or solution category, though they may not know you | Medium | Connect the topic they explored to a useful point of view or resource |
| **Third party: market and company changes** | Hiring, funding, a new leader, expansion, product news, or another observable business change | Their situation changed; a related need is possible | Higher | Research the likely consequence before deciding whether outreach is justified |

This is **our operating model for prioritizing outreach**, not a universal data-ownership taxonomy. Other providers may classify research on an external website as third-party intent data. We use the middle group to distinguish *category engagement* from broad company news because those two observations support very different conversations.

### First-party signals: they engaged with you

First-party signals happen on your own properties or through your own content and campaigns. A prospect registers for a webinar, returns to a service page, downloads a guide, replies to an email, or engages with a founder's post.

These signals can be valuable because the person has encountered your company. But their strength varies. A newsletter signup is different from repeated visits to a pricing or service page. A single anonymous page view may tell you little, and sometimes you can identify only the company, not the individual. The right question is: **what did they engage with, and what problem does that content address?**

The limitation is volume. If few relevant buyers encounter your company, there will be few first-party signals to act on. That is why content, executive thought leadership, paid distribution, and useful resources matter to an outbound system: they create more opportunities for the right buyers to discover and engage with you.

### Second-party signals: they engaged with your category

Someone may be researching a solution long before they find your website. They attend an event about project management software, participate in a relevant community, compare tools on a review site, or join a discussion about a recurring problem you solve.

That activity suggests **category interest**, not awareness of your company. It gives you a more specific starting point than a generic company trigger. For a project management vendor, engagement with a discussion about coordinating cross-functional projects can support a useful conversation about workflow visibility. It does not justify pretending the person asked to hear from that vendor.

The signal is only as useful as the data behind it. Check what actually happened, whether the person or account fits your market, how recent the activity was, and whether your team has a legitimate way to use that information.

### Third-party signals: their circumstances changed

Funding rounds, hiring plans, new executives, and expansion news are easy to find at scale. They can reveal timing or a possible change in priorities. They rarely reveal category interest on their own.

Consider a company hiring a data scientist. That does not automatically mean it needs a new data platform. A funding announcement does not automatically mean it needs marketing services. Yet many outbound campaigns treat those events as if they were buying intent and send the same congratulatory pitch to everyone on the list.

These signals work best as **context to investigate**, especially when combined with stronger evidence. A new revenue leader plus a stated plan to enter a new market plus engagement with your content gives you a more credible reason to act than the job change alone. A public event can still be highly useful when your experience shows a clear link to the problem you solve.

## A signal is a clue, not a sales instruction

Before a signal triggers outreach, we ask five questions:

1. **Fit:** Is this a company and buyer we can actually help?
2. **Relevance:** Does the observation point to a specific problem we solve, or are we inventing the connection?
3. **Confidence:** Do we know what happened, when it happened, and who or which account it relates to?
4. **Timing:** Is the observation still current enough to matter?
5. **Action:** What would be useful to this buyer now: a message, a resource, an ad, a call, or no outreach yet?

The answer may be to wait. Signal-based outbound should make your team more selective, not give it a new excuse to send at volume.

## How signal-based outbound works in practice

Suppose you sell software that helps companies manage complex projects.

- A company posts several project-management roles. That is a broad third-party change. It may be worth researching, but it does not establish a software need.
- A leader from that company joins a discussion about project visibility at an industry event. Now you have category engagement and a clearer problem to explore.
- The same account later visits your guide on project handoffs and signs up for a webinar. They have engaged with you directly. You can follow up on the topic they chose, provided the account fits and the contact context supports it.

The response should change as the evidence changes. The hiring event might place an account on a watchlist. Category engagement might justify sharing a useful point of view. Direct engagement with a relevant topic might justify a timely, personal follow-up. None of those steps requires claiming to know the buyer's budget or purchase timeline.

Operationally, the system needs to capture the event, match it to the right account, enrich the record, check fit, select a play, and preserve the reason for outreach in the CRM. When a buyer replies, sales should see the signal, the message, the response, and the next owner together. Otherwise, the signal disappears at the handoff.

## Where most teams leave value on the table

Many teams start with the easiest signals to buy or scrape: funding, hiring, and news. Those events produce plenty of names. They do not necessarily produce many good reasons to talk.

Our approach is to prioritize first-party engagement and relevant category activity, then use broader market changes to add context or expand coverage. We also work on the supply side of the problem. If your first-party signal volume is too low, the answer is not only a better intent-data feed. It may be a stronger flow of useful content, more distribution, and more executive or employee voices reaching the buyers you want to engage.

That is how content and outbound can support each other. Content gives buyers a reason to pay attention. Their engagement gives the outbound team a more informed reason to start a conversation. The CRM and sales handoff make it possible to learn whether that conversation became a qualified opportunity.

## How to measure whether it works

Count outcomes by signal source and play, not only the number of signals collected. Useful measures include:

- How many good-fit accounts each signal source produces.
- How often a signal leads to a qualified conversation or opportunity.
- Time from observation to appropriate action.
- How many signals turn out to be false positives or stale.
- Pipeline created or influenced by each play, with attribution defined consistently.

If a high-volume third-party feed produces almost no qualified conversations, lower its priority. If a small category-specific audience consistently creates useful sales conversations, invest more in reaching that audience. The goal is to learn which observations predict a problem you can actually solve.

## The practical definition

Signal-based outbound is a system for deciding **who is worth contacting, why now, what to say, and what should happen next**. A signal is a verifiable observation, not a guaranteed buyer. Its value comes from how well it connects to a real problem, a fitting account, and a useful response.
